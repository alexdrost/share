Newsletter name options — ranked

Middle Market Weekly

recommended

Clean, descriptive, SEO-native. "Middle Market" is the exact keyword your audience uses to describe themselves (467 mentions in company descriptions). "Weekly" sets cadence expectations clearly. Pairs naturally with middlemarket.media as the publishing domain. Easy to find, easy to remember, easy to forward with "you should subscribe to this."

More precise — directly claims the sub-$100M deal space where 68% of your audience operates. Stronger differentiation from ACG's broader positioning. Slightly harder to say out loud repeatedly.

Deal Signal

Punchy, memorable, signals deal intelligence focus. Good for a PE/IB-only track. Loses the "middle market" SEO anchor. Better as a sub-brand or section name within Middle Market Weekly.

Directly leverages existing brand equity from the conference series. Strong for warm list launch. Risk: conflates the newsletter with the conference in a way that may limit growth beyond the existing community.

Voice and tone principles

Practitioner, not pundit

Write like a Managing Director writing to other Managing Directors. No explaining what a management buyout is. Assume 15+ years of deal experience. Never condescend.

Data over opinion

"Valuations in business services are compressing" is weak. "Business services multiples dropped from 7.2x to 6.4x EBITDA in Q3" is what gets forwarded. Every claim needs a number.

Short over long

This audience reads at 6am on a phone. Lead with the most important sentence. No preamble. Target under 600 words for the weekly digest. Every word earns its place or gets cut.

Repeatable structure — every Monday send

From: Middle Market Weekly <weekly@middlemarket.media>

To: [First Name] — [Title], [Firm]

5 deals worth knowing this week + manufacturing multiples are moving

Section 1 — The lead (~80 words, one sharp insight)

Something shifted in lower middle market manufacturing this week. Three closed deals in the $25M–$75M range printed at sub-6x EBITDA — meaningful compression from the 7.1x average in Q2. Two were operational turnarounds, one was a clean platform acquisition. What's driving it: rising debt service costs are forcing sellers to recalibrate expectations faster than buyers are moving. If you're on the buy side in industrials, the next 60 days look interesting.

Section 2 — Deal digest (5 notable closed/announced deals, ~200 words)

Five deals from the past 7 days. Format: [Buyer] acquired [Target] in [Sector] for approximately $[Size]. [One sentence of deal context: what made it notable — platform vs. add-on, unusual structure, interesting geography, notable advisor team]. Source linked. Each entry is 2–3 lines. Five strong is better than eight mediocre.

Section 3 — One deep read (~100 words + link)

One longer piece worth the reader's time — your own content on middlemarket.media, a report from Lincoln International, a GF Data release, or a sector analysis. Two sentences on why it matters. Then the link. No excerpts. No summarizing the whole thing. Trust your reader to click.

Section 4 — Rotating sector feature (~100 words)

Week 1: Manufacturing/Industrials. Week 2: Business Services. Week 3: Healthcare. Week 4: SaaS/Tech-Enabled. Matches your four top industry focus sectors from the contact data. One data point, one trend, one question to think about. Gives regular readers a reason to look forward to a specific week's issue.

Section 5 — Community note (~40 words)

A brief human moment. A notable hire. An upcoming event. A question you're hearing from deal professionals. Sometimes just: "Congrats to [Name] at [Firm] on closing [Deal]." This is what makes it feel like a community, not a content product.

Total word count target: 500–700 words

The Managing Director who opens this at 6:15am before a board call needs to be done in 4 minutes. Every word that doesn't earn its place gets cut. The goal is not to be comprehensive — it's to be the one newsletter they actually finish reading.

Issue #1 — special structure

Issue #1 sets the contract with the reader

Open with "why this exists": One honest paragraph: "You've been building deals in the lower middle market for years. We built this newsletter because the information you actually need — real deal data, sector intelligence, what's moving and why — is scattered, expensive, or buried behind membership walls. We're fixing that."
Introduce the format: Tell readers what to expect every Monday. One paragraph. Then prove it by running the full format in that same issue.
Name the founding members: "This first issue goes to 107 founding members who've been part of this community for 5+ years. Thank you for making it real." The number signals that real people are behind this.

The rule for this audience

Managing Directors get 200+ emails a day. They open newsletters that prove in the subject line that the content inside is worth 4 minutes. Specificity beats cleverness. A number beats a vague claim. A counterintuitive finding beats a neutral summary. Never use "In this week's issue..." as a subject line.

Type 1 — data-specific (highest open rate for this audience)

Pattern: [specific number] + [specific claim]

Manufacturing multiples dropped to 5.9x — 3 deals that explain why

Specific number + specific deal count + implied insight. MD thinks "I need to know this before my next call."

Pattern: [data point] + [implication as question]

Business services close rates fell 18% in Q3. Buyers or sellers?

Data point creates urgency, question creates a reason to open and find the answer.

Pattern: [deal count] + [sector] + [time frame]

7 healthcare deals closed in the Midwest last week. Here's the breakdown.

Geographic specificity speaks directly to the Midwest-concentrated audience.

Type 2 — counterintuitive (drives curiosity opens)

Pattern: [unexpected finding] stated flatly

The best time to sell your industrial business might be right now

Goes against prevailing narrative. MD thinks "everyone says wait — why does this say sell?"

Pattern: [named firm type] + [surprising behavior]

Why independent sponsors are winning deals PE firms are passing on

Names two specific audience segments and implies a competitive dynamic relevant to both sides.

Type 3 — community / insider (highest forward rate)

Pattern: [community signal] + [specific detail]

Three firms in our network just closed platform deals in value-added distribution

"Our network" creates belonging. Specific sector creates relevance. People forward this to sector colleagues.

Pattern: [named individual] + [insight hook]

Alan Cuthbertson on what he looks for before writing a check

Uses a real name from the community (your 6× conference attendee). His peers will open it immediately.

Never use / always include

Never use

"In this week's issue..." · "What's happening in middle market M&A" · "Your weekly newsletter is here" · "Don't miss this week's top stories" · Emojis as the first character · Questions that answer themselves

Always include

A specific number or deal detail · Sector specificity (manufacturing, healthcare, etc.) · Something a non-deal professional would not understand · Enough information to know it's worth 4 minutes of time

Every cornerstone SEO article ends with a "Get the full data in Middle Market Weekly — free, every Monday" module. The annual report, the comp survey, the sector deal digests — all gated behind email capture. This is the flywheel: SEO → organic traffic → email capture → subscribers share content → more backlinks → better SEO. Build this system once, let it compound.

5

Referral program — leverage forwarding behavior (month 3+)

This audience forwards things they find genuinely useful. Build a lightweight referral mechanic in Beehiiv: each subscriber gets a personal referral link. 3 referrals = early access to the next data report. 10 referrals = named acknowledgment in the issue. The 514 power users are your referral engine — they each know 10–20 other deal professionals personally. A 10% referral rate from this cohort alone is 50 warm-introduction new subscribers.

List hygiene — critical for deliverability

Sending to an unengaged list destroys your sender reputation and kills deliverability for everyone. Before first send: validate all emails with ZeroBounce or NeverBounce. After 3 sends: suppress anyone who hasn't opened. After 8 sends: run a re-engagement campaign, then suppress non-responders permanently. A 5,000-subscriber list at 40% open rate is worth more than a 15,000-subscriber list at 8%.

Recommended tech stack

ESP: Beehiiv — primary recommendation

recommended

Purpose-built for newsletter businesses. Built-in subscriber referral program, segmentation, paid newsletter tiers, ad network access, and analytics dashboard. The business model alignment is the key advantage — Beehiiv is designed for newsletters that want to monetize through sponsorships and subscriptions. Pricing scales with list size, not features locked behind paywalls. Used by Morning Brew alumni and hundreds of B2B newsletters.

Alternative: Substack

Better for building a personal brand around a single writer. Strong discovery network. Weakness: limited segmentation — can't send different content to PE vs. IB subscribers easily. Paid tier is Substack-branded. Good for a solo editorial voice; less ideal as a brand-owned media property.

Alternative: Kit (ConvertKit)

Best-in-class email automation and segmentation. Excellent for building complex sequences like the founding member flow. Weaker on newsletter-native features like referral programs and paid subscriptions. Best if automation depth is the priority.

What winning looks like at 90 days

Success isn't just subscriber counts. It's when a Managing Director forwards your newsletter to a portfolio company CEO with "you should read this." It's when a law firm reaches out to ask about sponsorship without you pitching them. It's when you see your deal data cited in someone else's deal memo. Those moments mean the brand has become part of the operating vocabulary of the lower middle market — and at that point the newsletter becomes the single most valuable asset in the entire domain portfolio.
