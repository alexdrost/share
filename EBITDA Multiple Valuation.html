02

Where the multiple actually comes from

The single-rate formula above is a shortcut. Underneath, a real valuation is a discounted cash flow: project the cash for a handful of years, then estimate a terminal value for everything after. That terminal value is usually built one of two ways — a perpetuity-growth formula (1 ÷ (r − g) on the terminal rate) or a market-based exit multiple drawn from comparable deals. They're related, but they aren't the same thing: the exit multiple is anchored to market evidence, not derived mechanically from r and g.

Here's the part that trips people up: the g in the formula is terminal growth — the rate the business sustains in steady state, forever. High growth in the early forecast years is fine and normal. The problem is carrying a high rate like 15% as the permanent assumption for a mature business — that's generally not credible, and often incompatible with the discount rate. What's real is a front-loaded profile: several strong years that taper to a normal long-run rate. Toggle between a credible horizon and an over-aggressive flat one.

03

Where technology disruption hits

Disruption doesn't invent a new formula — it changes the inputs. Automation pressure lands on specific parts of a business, and each one routes back to either growth or risk. The danger isn't a single shock; it's several inputs drifting the wrong way at once, widening the spread and compressing the multiple.

affects growth · g

Commodity output gets repriced

Work whose value is the deliverable — standardized returns, bookkeeping, document production — may see pricing pressure as automation lowers the cost to produce it. How far depends on regulation, workflow integration, liability, and switching costs. The hit can land on growth, margins, or both.

commodity revenue share ↑  →  g and/or margins ↓
affects risk · r

Revenue durability gets questioned

A buyer underwriting future cash flows has to ask how much of today's revenue survives the next few years of capability gains. Greater uncertainty can lead buyers to demand a higher return — which raises the discount rate used in valuation, before any revenue is actually lost.

Or: capacity to serve expands

Pointed the other way, the same tools let a firm deliver more frequent, higher-value touchpoints at the same headcount — advisory and judgment work scales. That can show up as growth, and also through stronger margins and retention.

affects risk · r

Delivery stops depending on scarce people

Encoding firm knowledge reduces reliance on a thin, expensive labor pool and on any single rainmaker. More transferable, less key-person-dependent cash flows read as lower risk.

owner / staffing dependence ↓  →  r ↓

The split is the point: disruption tends to push the commodity-output firm and the trust-and-judgment firm in different directions — and many firms feel both at once, pressure on commodity work alongside expansion in advisory. Same technology, different net effect on the spread — so it can widen the gap between multiples rather than moving them together.

04

Timing and the macro tide

Firm-level quality sets where you sit relative to the market. But the market's overall level is heavily influenced by forces outside any single business — and those tend to move the discount rate. Demand has been historically high; the question worth holding is what pulls it back, and how much of that is outside your control.

Where the market is now
Elevated demand, compressed spreads
Heavy capital chasing low-capex, recurring-revenue professional services has bid multiples up. In a perpetuity framing, a high multiple implies a narrow spread between discount rate and growth — the fragile zone in the calculator above.
Why that's a timing question
The premium is borrowed from low rates
Much of today's multiple sits on a low cost of capital and abundant buyers. Both are cyclical. When financing conditions tighten, required returns often rise across the market — even for strong firms, though not all at the same pace.
  • drives r ↑

    Interest rates rise

    The risk-free rate is the floor under every discount rate. When rates climb, required returns tend to rise broadly and multiples compress — one of the most direct macro levers on valuation, though credit spreads, sector risk, and financing availability matter too.

  • drives r ↑

    Alternative opportunities get more attractive

    Buyers price every deal against what else they could do with the money. When safer or higher-returning assets appear — a rotation toward hard-asset sectors, sometimes called the "halo trade" — capital can flow out of professional services, pushing up the return required on what's left.

  • drives r ↑

    Buyer pool thins

    Today's multiples partly reflect roll-up competition bidding against each other. If acquirers pull back — over financing costs or disruption fears — fewer bidders often lowers clearing prices, which the market effectively reads as a higher required return.

  • drives g ↓

    Growth expectations reset

    If the market decides a category's organic growth has structurally slowed — disruption being the obvious trigger — the terminal g falls. Combined with any of the rate moves above, that's compression from both sides of the spread at once.

Near-term, market-wide repricing is often driven more by macro forces than by operations — rates and the cost of capital act on r and tend to move the whole market. Firm-level quality still matters: it shapes your relative resilience and how much buyer interest you hold when the tide goes out. Worth knowing which is which when the question is when, not just how much.
