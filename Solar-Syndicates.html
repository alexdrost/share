A guide from Hall CPA, PLLC

You were promised your solar investment would offset your active income. Here's what the tax code actually says.

A growing number of high-income taxpayers are being pitched solar investments that promise to wipe out their W-2 or business income through depreciation deductions and a 30% investment tax credit. The promotion is consistent and the math sounds clean. But for most investors in these structures, the tax benefits won't survive an IRS audit — and the consequences are significantly worse than just losing the deduction.

This page is written by a CPA, not a sales pitch. It explains what the law actually requires, walks through what's gone wrong in the tax cases that have already been decided, and gives you tools to assess your own situation before you sign — or, if you've already invested, to understand your exposure.

The 60-second version

The investment tax credit and accelerated depreciation on solar equipment can absolutely be claimed against active income — but only if the investor materially participates in the underlying business. For nearly every individual investor in a syndicated solar deal, that bar isn't cleared.

When material participation isn't met, both the credit and the depreciation become passive — meaning they can only offset passive income from other passive activities. They can't offset W-2 wages, business income, or capital gains. For most investors in these structures, that's the entire point of the deal evaporating.

Three patterns recur in the deals that have lost in court:

  • The promoter, not the investor, runs day-to-day operations.
  • The investor's "hours" consist of investor-level activity (reviewing reports, signing documents, occasional site visits) — which under federal regulation does not count toward material participation.
  • The structure is documented as a sale-and-leaseback between the investor's LLC and a promoter affiliate, with little independent economic substance.

If any of these describe a deal you're being pitched — or one you've already entered — the rest of this page is worth reading carefully.

What promoters tell investors versus what the tax code says

The pitches Hall CPA has reviewed are remarkably consistent across unrelated promoters. Below are four claims that come up nearly every time, and what the underlying authorities actually say.

The promoter saysThe tax code says
"You can claim the 30% investment tax credit and depreciation against your active income — your W-2, your business income, all of it." These benefits are available against active income only if the activity is a § 162 trade or business in which the investor materially participates under § 469. When that's not met, both the credit and the depreciation are passive and can only offset passive income. (IRC §§ 38, 48E, 469)
"Just spend 100 hours a year managing the project. You'll meet the 100-hour-and-more-than-anyone-else test." Under Treas. Reg. § 1.469-5T(f)(2)(ii)(A), hours spent in an investor capacity — reviewing financials, signing documents, monitoring operations in a non-managerial capacity — don't count toward material participation. The 100-hour test also requires the investor to put in more hours than any other individual, including the third-party manager who actually runs the project.
"Set up an LLC. Once you own the LLC, you're managing your own business." Forming an LLC doesn't establish a § 162 trade or business. The Tax Court has repeatedly held that owning an entity that owns income-producing property — without genuine operational involvement — is investor activity, not trade or business activity. (Higgins v. Commissioner, 312 U.S. 212; Olsen v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2021-41)
"The IRS hasn't challenged this structure." The IRS has challenged the structure, and the courts have agreed with the IRS. Olsen v. Commissioner — the leading published case on point — addressed more than 200 investors in a single program. The Tax Court ruled against the lead taxpayer on four independent grounds; the Tenth Circuit affirmed.

The four hurdles every solar deduction has to clear

The way the white paper version of this discussion is usually framed — and the way most pitches treat it — focuses entirely on material participation. That's only one of four independent legal tests an investor's deal has to clear. Each one is dispositive on its own, meaning a deal can lose by failing any single hurdle.

In Olsen, the Tax Court ruled against the taxpayer on all four:

1

Was there a real § 162 trade or business?

Owning an LLC that owns equipment does not, by itself, create a trade or business. Higgins v. Commissioner, 312 U.S. 212 (1941).

2

Was there a profit motive independent of tax savings?

A transaction whose only path to profit runs through tax credits is vulnerable on this ground. Nickeson v. Commissioner, 962 F.2d 973 (10th Cir. 1992).

3

Was the property actually placed in service?

Property has to be operating and producing income (or held out to do so in a real, multi-party transaction) — a sale-and-leaseback to a promoter affiliate is not enough. Cooper v. Commissioner, 88 T.C. 84 (1987).

4

Did the investor materially participate?

Under § 469 and Treas. Reg. § 1.469-5T, only one of seven specific tests will do — and investor-level hours don't count.

Each is discussed in turn below.

Hurdle 1: Was there a real § 162 trade or business?

The Supreme Court's framework comes from Commissioner v. Groetzinger, 480 U.S. 23 (1987): an activity is a trade or business if (1) the taxpayer is involved with continuity and regularity, and (2) the primary purpose is to produce income.

Two earlier Supreme Court cases set the outer limits of what does not qualify:

  • Higgins v. Commissioner, 312 U.S. 212 (1941) — the taxpayer ran a full back office to manage his family's investments. The Court held that managing one's own investments, no matter how much time or staff is involved, is not a § 162 trade or business.
  • Deputy v. du Pont, 308 U.S. 488 (1940) — distinguished business expenses from investment expenses and held that managing one's own estate generally does not rise to the level of a § 162 trade or business.

In a solar syndicate where the promoter handles procurement, installation, customer acquisition, operations, and maintenance — and the investor's role is to provide capital and receive reports — the structure looks much more like the Higgins facts than the Groetzinger facts. In Olsen, the Tax Court found that signing pre-drafted lease documents, writing checks, and emailing the promoter "plainly does not rise to the level of a trade or business." (Olsen, T.C. Memo. 2021-41 at 27–28.)

A § 212 "production of income" activity is a related but distinct category. § 212 expenses are still subject to § 469's passive activity limitations and were further limited by § 67(g)'s suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions through 2025. The cleanest comparison: a triple-net lease is § 212 territory; an actively managed rental business with operational responsibility is § 162 territory. Most solar syndicate structures, despite the labeling, look more like the former.

Hurdle 2: Was there a profit motive independent of tax savings?

The hobby-loss rules under § 183 — and the broader sham-transaction doctrine — require that the activity have a real profit motive independent of the tax benefits. A transaction whose only path to profit runs through tax credits is vulnerable.

The Tenth Circuit in Olsen applied the Nickeson five-factor test to find that the taxpayer there lacked a profit motive:

  1. The marketing materials focused on expected tax benefits.
  2. The taxpayer paid a grossly inflated price without negotiating.
  3. The taxpayer didn't ask the seller about potential profitability.
  4. The taxpayer lacked control over the activities of the venture.
  5. The taxpayer had no expertise, and his only "advisors" were the promoters.

A separate Ninth Circuit case, Sacks v. Commissioner, 69 F.3d 982 (9th Cir. 1995), is sometimes cited by promoters for the proposition that an activity isn't a sham just because the only profit comes from a tax credit. The holding is real but narrower than promoters represent. The taxpayer in Sacks had at least some non-tax economic substance, and the court explicitly held that a profit motive cannot arise solely from a desire for a tax benefit. The Tenth Circuit in Olsen distinguished Sacks on this basis.

Hurdle 3: Was the property actually placed in service?

The investment tax credit under §§ 48 and 48E only attaches when the qualifying property is "placed in service." Under Cooper v. Commissioner, 88 T.C. 84 (1987), property held for leasing in a profit-motivated venture is placed in service when it is first held out for lease — but Cooper required a genuine multi-party transaction, not a sale-and-leaseback between an investor and the promoter.

In Olsen, the Tax Court held the lenses were never placed in service for three reasons:

  • The transaction was a sale-and-leaseback between the investor and the promoter, not a "genuine multiple party transaction."
  • The lenses were never held out for lease to anyone other than the promoter's affiliates.
  • The lenses were never operating; the project never made it past R&D after years of nominal "investment."

Many syndicate structures present substantively the same pattern: the investor's LLC purchases equipment, the same equipment is leased back to a promoter affiliate the same day, and the project either never produces revenue or produces only nominal contingent rent that depends on the project actually working.

Hurdle 4: Did the investor materially participate?

This is the hurdle most pitches focus on, and it's the one most investors think they understand. They generally don't.

Under § 469 and Treas. Reg. § 1.469-5T(a), an investor materially participates only if they meet at least one of seven tests:

  1. More than 500 hours during the year.
  2. Their participation is substantially all of the participation in the activity.
  3. More than 100 hours during the year, and not less than the participation of any other individual.
  4. Significant participation in the activity (more than 100 hours), with aggregate significant participation in all such activities exceeding 500 hours.
  5. Material participation for any 5 of the prior 10 tax years.
  6. Personal service activity material participation for any 3 prior tax years (limited to certain professions).
  7. Facts-and-circumstances regular, continuous, and substantial participation, plus more than 100 hours during the year.

Promoters typically point to test #3 — 100 hours plus more than any other individual. In a deal where a third-party manager runs operations full-time, this test is essentially impossible to meet. The manager's hours are the relevant comparator.

The tests are also subject to a critical qualifier under Treas. Reg. § 1.469-5T(f)(2)(ii)(A): work done by an individual in their capacity as an investor doesn't count toward material participation unless the individual is "directly involved in the day-to-day management or operations of the activity." Investor-level work is defined to include:

  • Studying and reviewing financial statements or reports on operations.
  • Preparing summaries or analyses of finances or operations for the individual's own use.
  • Monitoring the finances or operations of the activity in a non-managerial capacity.

This is exactly what most syndicate investors do. They review reports the promoter sends them, they get on quarterly calls, they sign annual documents. Under the regulation, those hours don't count.

What the courts have actually held

Five Tax Court decisions are the practical reference points for any analysis of these structures. The first three are not solar cases — they're the broader passive-activity decisions every solar case relies on. The last two are direct solar cases.

T.C. Memo. 1993-359

Toups v. Commissioner

Facts: A working engineer invested in a vacation cottage at Callaway Gardens in Georgia. The cottage was managed and marketed by Callaway Gardens under a rental management agreement. The taxpayer claimed 341 hours of activity related to the cottage — providing funds, preparing budgets, reviewing reports, and arranging the rental management.

Holding: The Tax Court held that all 341 hours were investor-level activity. The taxpayer was not involved in day-to-day operations. Losses were passive. The court noted that even an annual ownership meeting wouldn't have changed the analysis.

Lesson: A third-party manager who actually runs day-to-day operations can foreclose material participation no matter how many hours the investor logs.

T.C. Memo. 2015-24

Kline v. Commissioner

Facts: A pilot bought two yachts and contracted with a management company for marketing, customer payments, cleaning, and maintenance. Unlike the Toups taxpayer, Kline took on the bulk of marketing himself — to fellow pilots, where he had connections — and personally captained charters with friends who became customers.

Holding: The Tax Court held Kline materially participated. His marketing and day-to-day operational hours counted because he was directly involved in operations, not just oversight.

Lesson: It's possible to have a third-party manager and still materially participate, but the investor has to actually do operational work, not just direct it.

T.C. Memo. 2014-65

Tolin v. Commissioner

Facts: A taxpayer launched a thoroughbred breeding business in Louisiana to breed his champion racehorse. He claimed 850–900 hours per year. The IRS argued the hours were investor-level and the activity was passive.

Holding: The Tax Court held Tolin materially participated. Decisive facts: he personally promoted the horse to breeders, made multiple phone calls per day to interested parties, traveled extensively to promote in person, moved the horse between farms when he wasn't satisfied with care, and worked 12-hour days during promotional periods. The Court found his activity "exceeded what a normal horse owner and breeder would do."

Lesson: Investor-level activity transforms into material participation when the investor demonstrably performs operational work that a non-owner manager would otherwise have to do.

T.C. Memo. 2021-41 (aff'd 10th Cir. 2022)

Olsen v. Commissioner

Facts: The most directly on-point published decision. Preston Olsen, a Utah law firm partner, bought "solar lenses" from a promoter's affiliated entities between 2009 and 2014. The lenses were supposed to be components of a concentrated solar power system. Olsen formed an LLC to purchase the lenses, then immediately leased them back to one of the promoter's affiliates, who would pay $150 per lens per year in rent — but only if the system produced electricity. It never did. Olsen claimed depreciation deductions and § 48 energy credits that zeroed out his federal income tax in those years.

Holding: The Tax Court ruled against Olsen on four independent grounds:

  • No § 162 trade or business: the LLC had no bank account, books, employees, business plan, or customers.
  • No profit motive: Olsen never visited the site, couldn't identify which lenses were "his," and had no expertise.
  • Lenses never placed in service: the sale-and-leaseback wasn't a genuine multi-party transaction.
  • Passive activity: even if the first three were satisfied, Olsen didn't materially participate.

The Tenth Circuit affirmed. The federal district court overseeing the underlying enforcement action against the promoter issued a permanent injunction. More than 200 other investor cases were held pending the Olsen outcome.

Lesson: The structural pattern in Olsen — investor LLC, sale-leaseback, contingent rent, third-party operations, minimal investor involvement — is a fact pattern the courts have already addressed, repeatedly, with consistent results.

T.C. Memo. 2018-76

Golan v. Commissioner

Facts: A taxpayer purchased existing solar panels, claimed bonus depreciation and the energy credit, and offset active income. The IRS challenged the deductions and credits, but the IRS's notice of deficiency raised the wrong issues — § 179 expensing rather than § 469 passive activity rules — and didn't carry its burden of proof on the new issue under Tax Court Rule 142(a).

Holding: Golan won. But the win was procedural, not a substantive vindication of the structure.

Lesson: Practitioners reading Golan generally treat it as a reminder that documentation matters and that the IRS's procedural posture matters — not as a roadmap for surviving an audit.

The risks the pitch deck didn't cover

Even setting aside the four hurdles, syndicate structures carry risks that don't usually appear in promotional materials. These matter for any investor weighing a deal — and for any investor who's already in one.

5-year ITC recapture clock

The investment tax credit is not earned all at once. It vests over five years from the date the property is placed in service. If the property is sold, ceases operations, or is otherwise disposed of within that window, the credit is recaptured on the following schedule under § 50(a):

Time elapsed since placed in serviceRecapture percentage
Less than 1 year100%
1 to less than 2 years80%
2 to less than 3 years60%
3 to less than 4 years40%
4 to less than 5 years20%
5+ years0%

Recapture events for a syndicate investor include: selling the LLC interest, the project ceasing operations, the equipment being moved out of qualified use, and certain partnership restructurings. Reg. § 1.47-6 governs partnership-level recapture; a partner can be hit with recapture if their share of the partnership's profits is reduced during the recapture period, even without an outright sale.

In a deal where the project never operates as advertised — or where the promoter shuts down within five years — recapture compounds with the disallowance.

§ 49 vs. § 465 at-risk — two different regimes

There are two at-risk regimes under the Code, and they have different rules. Most pitches conflate them.

§ 49 at-risk governs how much of the project basis is eligible for the ITC. Under § 49, up to 80% of qualifying basis can be financed with qualified nonrecourse debt (or up to ~75% for level-payment loans) as long as the debt comes from a federal/state/local government agency or a lender regularly engaged in the business of lending. Most well-structured deals can satisfy this.

§ 465 at-risk governs how much of an investor's allocable losses can actually be deducted. Under § 465, an individual is at-risk only to the extent of cash or property contributed plus recourse debt for which they are personally liable. Nonrecourse debt — including the qualified nonrecourse debt that satisfies § 49 — generally does NOT increase the individual's § 465 at-risk amount for solar equipment (the qualified nonrecourse exception in § 465(b)(6) applies to real-property activities, not equipment).

The result: an investor can be told "your deal is 80% financed, you only need 20% cash," and still find that the depreciation deductions in excess of their cash investment are suspended at the partner level, awaiting either offsetting income or additional at-risk capital. Form 6198 is the reporting mechanism.

Bonus depreciation and the post-OBBBA depreciation profile

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, made changes to the depreciation profile of solar equipment that pre-OBBBA pitch decks don't reflect.

Pre-OBBBA, energy property was classified as 5-year MACRS under § 168(e)(3)(B)(vi) — a critical depreciation accelerator. OBBBA repealed that classification for energy property where construction began after December 31, 2024. Such property now depreciates under its underlying class life under standard MACRS, which is generally 7-year MACRS or longer for solar equipment.

OBBBA also restored 100% bonus depreciation for property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. The interaction between the bonus depreciation restoration and the elimination of 5-year MACRS classification for solar specifically is technical — investors with a deal that started construction in 2024 versus 2025 are looking at materially different depreciation profiles, and pitch decks created during the IRA window may not reflect the current rules.

Accuracy-related and gross valuation misstatement penalties

If the IRS disallows credits and deductions on audit, the deficiency is generally subject to a 20% accuracy-related penalty under § 6662 for negligence or substantial understatement of tax.

If the deal involved equipment purchased at a price more than 200% of its correct value (or claimed an amount more than 150% of correct value), the penalty escalates to 40% under § 6662(h) for a "gross valuation misstatement." This is the same penalty regime the IRS applied across the board in syndicated conservation easement cases. The reasonable-cause defense under § 6664(c) is available against the 20% penalty in many circumstances; for the 40% gross valuation misstatement penalty, the defense is significantly narrower.

Reportable transactions and Form 8886

The IRS designates certain transaction types as "listed transactions" that must be disclosed by every participant on Form 8886. Failure to disclose triggers § 6707A penalties of $100,000 per individual or $200,000 per entity per year of non-disclosure — separate from any underlying tax adjustment.

Solar syndicate transactions of the type discussed on this page have not been designated as listed transactions as of the publication date of this resource. They share structural features with syndicated conservation easement transactions, which the IRS designated as listed transactions in Notice 2017-10, but the IRS has not taken that step here. If listing did occur in the future, current investors would face retroactive disclosure obligations. The takeaway for investors weighing a deal today: the disclosure exposure is something to discuss with a tax advisor before signing.

Promoter audits and material advisor obligations

Promoters of abusive tax shelters face civil penalties under § 6700 of 50% of gross income derived from the promotion — including ongoing management and maintenance fees, not just upfront sales (per Davison v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2020-58). Material advisors face their own disclosure and list-maintenance requirements under §§ 6111 and 6112, with separate penalties under § 6707 for failures.

When a promoter audit progresses to the point of an injunction or guilty plea, several things happen that affect the underlying investors:

  • The IRS subpoenas the promoter's investor list under §§ 6111 / 6112.
  • Each investor on that list becomes a known IRS target.
  • The promoter typically can no longer assist investors with ongoing compliance — leaving investors without the operational support that was part of the original pitch.

For investors, the practical implication is that "the promoter said it's fine" provides no audit protection at all.

Why solar promotions are intensifying right now

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Pub. L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) created a hard cliff for the solar credit. For wind and solar facilities, the § 48E Clean Electricity Investment Credit terminates for property placed in service after December 31, 2027 — unless construction began on or before July 4, 2026, in which case the four-year continuity safe harbor applies.

That window, combined with the President's July 7, 2025 Executive Order directing Treasury to "strictly enforce" the termination and prevent "circumvention" of the beginning-of-construction rules, has compressed the runway for new solar projects substantially. IRS Notice 2025-42, issued September 2025, tightened the BOC rules in line with the Executive Order.

For investors, the practical consequence is that solar promoters know the credit window is closing. The pace and intensity of promotions in 2025 and 2026 reflects that pressure. From an investor's perspective, time pressure is a structural risk: it's the mechanism by which due diligence gets short-circuited and red flags get explained away.

If a promoter is creating urgency around a year-end deadline or "last chance" framing, that urgency exists because of the OBBBA cliff — and the urgency is exactly why a careful review of the structure is more important right now, not less.

What this means in your state

A federal disallowance of solar syndicate credits and deductions doesn't stay federal. Most states with an income tax start their calculation from federal AGI or federal taxable income, which means a federal change generally flows through to state. The magnitude of the state-level exposure depends on the state's income tax rate, its conformity to federal bonus depreciation, and a handful of state-specific quirks.

Ten red flags from cases that have already been decided

The factors below are drawn from the published opinions in Olsen, Toups, and the Nickeson line of cases. They're not the only indicators of a problematic structure, but they're the ones courts have repeatedly cited. None of them alone is dispositive. Several together describe a deal that closely resembles structures the courts have already ruled on.

  1. The LLC was set up by the promoter, using the promoter's documents. This was the structural pattern in Olsen.
  2. The purchase price for the equipment was significantly higher than its market value. Nickeson identified inflated, non-negotiated pricing as a profit-motive red flag.
  3. The lease back to the promoter (or a promoter affiliate) was signed the same day or shortly after the purchase. Under Cooper, this is the absence of a "genuine multi-party transaction."
  4. The promoter handles procurement, installation, end-user contracts, operations, and maintenance. Toups and Olsen both turned on this fact.
  5. Income from the project is contingent on the project working — and the project isn't working yet. Olsen received zero income through every audit year because the project never operated.
  6. The investor's time on the activity consists of phone calls, emails, signing documents, and reading reports. Reg. § 1.469-5T(f)(2)(ii) defines this as investor-level activity.
  7. The investor has not visited the project site, or has visited only once or twice. Compare Tolin, where the taxpayer's near-constant on-site presence was decisive.
  8. The marketing materials emphasize "tax-free" or "100% offset" of active income. Nickeson identified marketing focus on tax benefits as a profit-motive red flag.
  9. The promoter referred the investor to a specific CPA who has prepared returns for many other investors in the same program. This was the Olsen pattern. Return preparers in this position have their own § 6694 preparer-penalty exposure.
  10. The investor is being told they must commit before the end of the tax year, or before some imminent deadline. Time pressure prevents due diligence and is a structural feature of these promotions.

What a defensible solar investment actually looks like

Hall CPA is not anti-solar. Solar is a real industry, and well-structured solar investments produce real economic returns alongside legitimate tax benefits. This page is about distinguishing structures that survive scrutiny from structures that don't.

A defensible solar investment generally falls into one of four categories:

Real operating business with materially-participating owners

The investor (or their employees) handles procurement, site selection, customer acquisition, ongoing operations, and maintenance. The business has employees, books, a real office. Tolin v. Commissioner is the analogue: the taxpayer didn't just own the asset, he ran the business.

Properly structured tax equity partnerships

Sophisticated tax equity transactions exist where a corporate or institutional investor takes the credits in exchange for capital contributions, with the developer continuing to operate the project. These are routinely closed by tax equity practitioners and survive examination because they have genuine economic substance, real allocation of operational responsibility, and arm's-length pricing. They're also generally not the structures pitched to high-income individuals as "100 hours a year and you're done."

Direct ownership of solar by an operating business

A business with employees and real operations buys solar equipment to power its own facilities (or to generate sellable power as part of its operations). The credit and depreciation flow through the business; the owners get the benefit through their interest in the business. Material participation isn't an issue because the underlying business has genuine operations.

Passive investor accepting passive treatment

The cleanest answer for many high-income investors is to invest in a solar project and accept that the credits and losses are passive — using them only to offset passive income from other passive activities. This is legally bulletproof, requires far less involvement from the investor, but produces a smaller absolute tax benefit than the active-treatment pitches promise. For investors without meaningful passive income, the deal often doesn't pencil.

The pattern this page warns against is the one where (a), (b), or (c) is misrepresented as something it isn't — and where individual investors are told they can claim active treatment despite having no operational role.
