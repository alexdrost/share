You were promised your solar investment would offset your active income. Here's what the tax code actually says.

High-income taxpayers are being pitched solar deals that promise to wipe out W-2 or business income through depreciation and a 30% credit. The math sounds clean. For most investors in these structures, the tax benefits won't survive an IRS audit — and the consequences are worse than just losing the deduction.

Written by a CPA, not a sales pitch. Walk through what the law requires, what's gone wrong in cases that have been decided, and use the interactive tools to assess your own situation — before you sign, or to understand exposure if you've already invested.

The thesis in 90 seconds

The investment tax credit and accelerated depreciation on solar equipment can absolutely be claimed against active income — but only if the investor materially participates in the underlying business. For nearly every individual investor in a syndicated solar deal, that bar isn't cleared.

When material participation isn't met, both the credit and depreciation become passive — they can only offset other passive income. They can't offset W-2 wages, business income, or capital gains. For most investors, that's the entire point of the deal evaporating.

Three patterns recur in deals that have lost in court

If any of these describe your deal, the rest of the page is worth reading carefully.

The promoter, not the investor, runs operations.

Procurement, installation, customer acquisition, ongoing operations, and maintenance — all the promoter's. The investor's role is to provide capital and receive reports.

02

The investor's "hours" are investor-capacity hours.

Reviewing reports, signing documents, occasional site visits — Reg. § 1.469-5T(f)(2)(ii) explicitly excludes those hours from material participation.

PROMOTER VS. CODE

What promoters tell investors versus what the tax code says

The pitches Hall CPA has reviewed are remarkably consistent across unrelated promoters. Below are four claims that come up nearly every time, and what the underlying authorities actually say.

The promoter says
The tax code says
"You can claim the 30% investment tax credit and depreciation against your active income — your W-2, your business income, all of it."
These benefits are available against active income only if the activity is a § 162 trade or business in which the investor materially participates under § 469. When that's not met, both the credit and depreciation are passive and can only offset passive income. (IRC §§ 38, 48E, 469)
"Just spend 100 hours a year managing the project. You'll meet the 100-hour-and-more-than-anyone-else test."
Under Treas. Reg. § 1.469-5T(f)(2)(ii)(A), hours spent in an investor capacity — reviewing financials, signing documents, monitoring operations in a non-managerial capacity — don't count. Test 3 also requires more hours than any other individual, including the third-party manager actually running the project.
"Set up an LLC. Once you own the LLC, you're managing your own business."
Forming an LLC doesn't establish a § 162 trade or business. Owning an entity that owns income-producing property — without genuine operational involvement — is investor activity, not trade or business activity. (Higgins v. Commissioner, 312 U.S. 212; Olsen v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2021-41)
"The IRS hasn't challenged this structure."
The IRS has challenged it, and the courts agreed with the IRS. Olsen v. Commissioner — the leading published case on point — addressed more than 200 investors in a single program. The Tax Court ruled against the lead taxpayer on four independent grounds; the Tenth Circuit affirmed.
02 — THE FOUR HURDLES

Every solar deduction has to clear four hurdles

Most pitches focus only on material participation. That's one of four independent legal tests. Each is dispositive on its own — a deal can lose by failing any single one.

In Olsen, the Tax Court ruled against the taxpayer on all four. Click any hurdle below to expand:

Hurdle 01: Was there a real § 162 trade or business?

The Supreme Court's framework comes from Commissioner v. Groetzinger, 480 U.S. 23 (1987): an activity is a trade or business if (1) the taxpayer is involved with continuity and regularity, and (2) the primary purpose is to produce income.

Two earlier Supreme Court cases set the outer limits of what does not qualify:

Higgins v. Commissioner, 312 U.S. 212 (1941) — the taxpayer ran a full back office to manage his family's investments. The Court held that managing one's own investments, no matter how much time or staff is involved, is not a § 162 trade or business.

Deputy v. du Pont, 308 U.S. 488 (1940) — distinguished business expenses from investment expenses; managing one's own estate generally does not rise to the level of a § 162 trade or business.

In a solar syndicate where the promoter handles procurement, installation, customer acquisition, operations, and maintenance — and the investor's role is to provide capital and receive reports — the structure looks much more like the Higgins facts than the Groetzinger facts. In Olsen, the Tax Court found that signing pre-drafted lease documents, writing checks, and emailing the promoter "plainly does not rise to the level of a trade or business."

Hurdle 02: Was there a profit motive independent of tax savings?

The hobby-loss rules under § 183 — and the broader sham-transaction doctrine — require that the activity have a real profit motive independent of the tax benefits. A transaction whose only path to profit runs through tax credits is vulnerable.

The Tenth Circuit in Olsen applied the Nickeson five-factor test:

  1. The marketing materials focused on expected tax benefits.
  2. The taxpayer paid a grossly inflated price without negotiating.
  3. The taxpayer didn't ask the seller about potential profitability.
  4. The taxpayer lacked control over the activities of the venture.
  5. The taxpayer had no expertise; his only "advisors" were the promoters.

Sacks v. Commissioner, 69 F.3d 982 (9th Cir. 1995), is sometimes cited by promoters for the proposition that an activity isn't a sham just because the only profit comes from a tax credit. The holding is real but narrower than promoters represent — the taxpayer in Sacks had at least some non-tax economic substance, and the court held that a profit motive cannot arise solely from a desire for a tax benefit.

Hurdle 03: Was the property actually placed in service?

The investment tax credit under §§ 48 and 48E only attaches when the qualifying property is "placed in service." Under Cooper v. Commissioner, 88 T.C. 84 (1987), property held for leasing in a profit-motivated venture is placed in service when it is first held out for lease — but Cooper required a genuine multi-party transaction, not a sale-and-leaseback between an investor and the promoter.

In Olsen, the Tax Court held the lenses were never placed in service for three reasons:

  • The transaction was a sale-and-leaseback between the investor and the promoter, not a "genuine multiple party transaction."
  • The lenses were never held out for lease to anyone other than the promoter's affiliates.
  • The lenses were never operating; the project never made it past R&D after years of nominal investment.

Many syndicate structures present substantively the same pattern: investor LLC purchases equipment, the same equipment is leased back to a promoter affiliate the same day, and the project either never produces revenue or produces only nominal contingent rent.

Hurdle 04: Did the investor materially participate?

Most pitches focus here, and most investors think they understand it. They generally don't. Under § 469 and Treas. Reg. § 1.469-5T(a), an investor materially participates only if they meet at least one of seven tests:

  1. More than 500 hours during the year.
  2. Substantially all participation in the activity.
  3. More than 100 hours and more than any other individual.
  4. Significant participation in the activity (>100 hours) with aggregate >500 hours.
  5. Material participation for any 5 of the prior 10 years.
  6. Personal service activity material participation for any 3 prior years.
  7. Facts-and-circumstances regular, continuous, substantial participation, plus >100 hours.

Promoters typically point to test #3. But Reg. § 1.469-5T(f)(2)(ii)(A) excludes work done in your capacity as an investor unless you're directly involved in day-to-day management. Investor-level work explicitly includes reviewing financials, preparing summaries, and monitoring the activity in a non-managerial capacity. That's exactly what most syndicate investors do.

04 — HIDDEN RISKS

The risks the pitch deck didn't cover

Even setting aside the four hurdles, syndicate structures carry risks that don't usually appear in promotional materials. Click any topic below to expand:

The investment tax credit is not earned all at once. It vests over five years from the date the property is placed in service. If the property is sold, ceases operations, or is otherwise disposed of within that window, the credit is recaptured under § 50(a):

Recapture Clock

Where are you in the 5-year window?

Calculates recapture under § 50(a). Doesn't account for partner-level recapture (Reg. § 1.47-6), partial dispositions, or other technical events. Actual exposure depends on facts.

Recapture events for a syndicate investor include: selling the LLC interest, the project ceasing operations, the equipment being moved out of qualified use, and certain partnership restructurings. In a deal where the project never operates as advertised — or where the promoter shuts down within five years — recapture compounds with the disallowance.

There are two at-risk regimes under the Code, and they have different rules. Most pitches conflate them.

§ 49 at-risk governs how much of the project basis is eligible for the ITC. Up to 80% of qualifying basis can be financed with qualified nonrecourse debt as long as the debt comes from a federal/state/local government agency or a lender regularly engaged in the business of lending. Most well-structured deals can satisfy this.

§ 465 at-risk governs how much of an investor's allocable losses can actually be deducted. An individual is at-risk only to the extent of cash or property contributed plus recourse debt for which they are personally liable. Nonrecourse debt — including the qualified nonrecourse debt that satisfies § 49 — generally does NOT increase the individual's § 465 at-risk amount for solar equipment (the qualified nonrecourse exception in § 465(b)(6) applies to real-property activities, not equipment).

Result: an investor told "your deal is 80% financed, you only need 20% cash" can still find that depreciation deductions in excess of cash investment are suspended at the partner level. Form 6198 is the reporting mechanism.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, made changes to the depreciation profile of solar equipment that pre-OBBBA pitch decks don't reflect.

Pre-OBBBA, energy property was classified as 5-year MACRS under § 168(e)(3)(B)(vi). OBBBA repealed that classification for energy property where construction began after December 31, 2024. Such property now depreciates under its underlying class life — generally 7-year MACRS or longer for solar equipment.

OBBBA also restored 100% bonus depreciation for property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. The interaction with the elimination of 5-year MACRS classification is technical — investors with a deal that started construction in 2024 vs. 2025 are looking at materially different depreciation profiles.

If the IRS disallows credits and deductions on audit, the deficiency is generally subject to a 20% accuracy-related penalty under § 6662 for negligence or substantial understatement of tax.

If the deal involved equipment purchased at a price more than 200% of correct value (or claimed amount more than 150% of correct value), the penalty escalates to 40% under § 6662(h) for a "gross valuation misstatement." This is the same penalty regime the IRS applied across the board in syndicated conservation easement cases. The reasonable-cause defense under § 6664(c) is available against the 20% penalty in many circumstances; for the 40% penalty, the defense is significantly narrower.

Certain transaction types are designated as "listed transactions" that must be disclosed on Form 8886. Failure to disclose triggers § 6707A penalties of $100,000 per individual or $200,000 per entity per year — separate from any underlying tax adjustment.

Solar syndicate transactions of the type discussed on this page have not been designated as listed transactions as of the publication date. They share structural features with syndicated conservation easement transactions, which the IRS designated in Notice 2017-10. If listing did occur in the future, current investors would face retroactive disclosure obligations.

Promoters of abusive tax shelters face civil penalties under § 6700 of 50% of gross income derived from the promotion — including ongoing management and maintenance fees, not just upfront sales (per Davison v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2020-58). Material advisors face their own disclosure and list-maintenance requirements under §§ 6111 and 6112.

When a promoter audit progresses to injunction or guilty plea, several things happen that affect the underlying investors:

  • The IRS subpoenas the promoter's investor list under §§ 6111 / 6112.
  • Each investor on that list becomes a known IRS target.
  • The promoter typically can no longer assist investors with ongoing compliance.

"The promoter said it's fine" provides no audit protection at all.

05 — WHY NOW

Why solar promotions are intensifying right now

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Pub. L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) created a hard cliff for the solar credit. Promoters know the window is closing — and time pressure is the mechanism by which due diligence gets short-circuited.

08 — WHAT'S DEFENSIBLE

What a defensible solar investment actually looks like

Hall CPA is not anti-solar. Solar is a real industry, and well-structured solar investments produce real returns alongside legitimate tax benefits. This page is about distinguishing structures that survive scrutiny from those that don't.

A

Real operating business with materially-participating owners

The investor (or their employees) handles procurement, site selection, customer acquisition, ongoing operations, and maintenance. The business has employees, books, a real office. Tolin v. Commissioner is the analogue: the taxpayer didn't just own the asset, he ran the business.

B

Properly structured tax equity partnerships

Sophisticated tax equity transactions where a corporate or institutional investor takes the credits in exchange for capital, with the developer continuing to operate. These survive examination because they have genuine economic substance, real allocation of operational responsibility, and arm's-length pricing — and they're generally not the structures pitched to high-income individuals as "100 hours a year and you're done."

C

Direct ownership of solar by an operating business

A business with employees and real operations buys solar to power its own facilities (or to generate sellable power as part of operations). The credit and depreciation flow through the business; owners get the benefit through their interest. Material participation isn't an issue because the underlying business has genuine operations.

D

Passive investor accepting passive treatment

Invest in a solar project and accept that the credits and losses are passive — using them only to offset passive income from other passive activities. Legally bulletproof, requires far less involvement, but produces a smaller absolute tax benefit than the active-treatment pitches promise. For investors without meaningful passive income, the deal often doesn't pencil.

The pattern this page warns against is the one where (A), (B), or (C) is misrepresented as something it isn't — and where individual investors are told they can claim active treatment despite having no operational role.
